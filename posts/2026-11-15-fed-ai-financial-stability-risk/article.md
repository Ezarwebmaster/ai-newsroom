---
title: "Federal Reserve flags AI as top financial stability risk as advanced computing debt passes $680 billion"
dek: "In its November 13, 2026 Financial Stability Report, the U.S. central bank said 87% of surveyed market contacts cited AI as a salient risk and estimated off-balance-sheet data center leases and commitments above $3 trillion."
---

Artificial intelligence has overtaken geopolitical risks and an oil shock as the most cited near-term risk among market contacts surveyed for the Federal Reserve Board's semiannual Financial Stability Report, published on November 13, 2026.[^1][^2][^3][^6]
In a September and October 2026 survey by Federal Reserve Bank of New York staff of 23 market contacts at banks, broker-dealers, investment funds, and advisory firms, 87% named AI as a salient risk, up from 50% in May 2026 and 30% in fall 2025.[^2][^3][^4]
Alongside the survey, the central bank reported that debt issuance tied to advanced computing infrastructure exceeded $680 billion through October 2026, roughly five times the $130 billion issued in all of 2023.[^2][^3][^5]

## Three AI worries and the New York Fed survey

The report, reflecting data as of October 28, 2026, summarizes the Board's framework for assessing U.S. financial system resilience and notes that its market outreach box does not represent the official views of the Board or the New York Fed.[^1][^3]
Surveyed contacts grouped AI risks into three areas: elevated equity valuations vulnerable to a market correction if earnings disappoint, cyberattacks by state actors or damage caused by frontier AI models themselves, and "complex forms of AI-related financing".[^2][^3]
Behind AI at 87%, respondents cited a risk-asset correction at 52%, higher long-term rates at 48%, cyberattacks at 43%, and fiscal debt sustainability at 43%, while private credit mentions fell from spring 2026, when geopolitics and an oil shock led and AI ranked third.[^2][^4]

In its own summary of that outreach, the Board noted that "a larger percentage of respondents mentioned risks from high interest rates than had done so in the spring survey".[^6]
Respondents rated cyberattacks and fiscal debt sustainability, rather than AI, as the risks with the most severe potential consequences.[^6]

## $680 billion in debt and $3 trillion in off-balance-sheet commitments

In a box titled "Leverage in the Data Center Buildout," the Fed found that advanced computing borrowing rose to about 31% of nonfinancial investment-grade corporate debt issuance in 2026, up from under 6% in 2023.[^2][^3][^5]
It estimates outstanding public debt tied to advanced computing at over $1.1 trillion as of October 2026, noting that private credit deals make the total harder to measure as financing shifts beyond corporate bonds into private debt, structured credit, and project finance.[^2][^3][^5]
<!-- add role="reviser" model="Anthropic Claude Opus 5.5" -->
The Fed said investment-grade technology companies issued most of this debt, and its issuance estimate leaves out private placements made under section 4(a)(2) of the Securities Act of 1933.[^6]
<!-- /add -->
Across seven firms—Alphabet, Amazon, Broadcom, Meta, Microsoft, Nvidia, and Oracle—undiscounted future leases that have not yet commenced exceed $1 trillion, and take-or-pay contracts, residual value guarantees, and similar commitments exceed $2 trillion.[^2][^3][^5]
<!-- add role="reviser" model="Anthropic Claude Opus 5.5" -->
Those lease and guarantee figures are undiscounted, and the report cautions that they "are not directly comparable with on-balance-sheet debt, which is discounted".[^6]
<!-- /add -->

## Circular financing, bank exposure and the 1990s telecom parallel

The report described a "circular financing" dynamic in which hyperscalers sign long leases that third-party developers use to borrow for construction, while graphics chip makers guarantee the residual value of chips used as collateral alongside equity stakes.[^2][^3][^4]
While those links could let large tech firms absorb downturn losses, the Fed warned their economic exposure "may be substantially larger than suggested by their on-balance-sheet liabilities" and that the interlinkages "create vulnerabilities that necessitate close monitoring".[^2][^3][^4]
High-yield bond funds, life insurers, business development companies, and private debt vehicles hold the riskier subordinated tranches of data center special purpose entities, while the largest U.S. bank holding companies hold close to $1 trillion in exposure evaluated in the annual supervisory stress test.[^2][^3]
<!-- add role="reviser" model="Anthropic Claude Opus 5.5" -->
The hyperscalers' and GPU makers' on-balance-sheet debt, by contrast, is held primarily by mutual funds, pension funds and insurance companies, according to the report.[^6]
The Board still described the banking sector as "sound and resilient overall", with banks reporting regulatory capital near historically high levels.[^6]
<!-- /add -->
Noting that hyperscaler capital spending scaled to gross domestic product may soon pass the late-1990s telecom buildout, with S&P 500 price-to-earnings ratios elevated partly by enthusiasm for "super intelligence (SI)" and hedge fund leverage at its highest level since 2013, the Fed warned an earnings downturn could trigger lower asset prices, tighter credit, and reduced capital spending.[^2][^3][^4]
<!-- add role="reviser" model="Anthropic Claude Opus 5.5" -->
Outside the data center box, the Board's overview said the ratio of business debt to gross domestic product continued a gradual decline but stayed in the upper quartile of its historical range.[^6]
It added: "Robust debt issuance by large technology companies may drive aggregate business debt higher over time."[^6]
