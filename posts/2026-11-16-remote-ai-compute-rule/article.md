title: "U.S. targets remote access to advanced AI chips through overseas data centers"
dek: "A Commerce Department rule adds licensing controls for advanced computing supplied remotely to certain foreign companies and for chip shipments to five countries."
---

The U.S. Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security announced an interim final rule in a release dated November 15, 2026, addressing remote access to advanced AI chips and servers through data centers in third countries.[^1]

The downloadable announcement bears that date, while the live BIS announcement is dated November 16, 2026.[^1][^5]
<!-- /add -->
The bureau says the measure is designed to prevent remote computing arrangements from circumventing existing restrictions on sensitive American technology.[^1]
For chip suppliers and data center operators, the rule ties licensing obligations to the computing capacity and ultimate users of a service, including indirect access.[^2]

## A threshold across connected facilities

The rule requires a license for covered exports, reexports or in-country transfers when the supplier knows the items are destined for a data center that provides or will provide more than 12 million aggregate total processing performance, or TPP, to or for companies headquartered in Macau or a Country Group D:5 destination, or whose ultimate parent is headquartered there.[^2]
The threshold can be reached at one facility or across linked data centers, including digitally connected facilities that share resources and workloads.[^2]
TechCrunch reports that the D:5 group includes China, Russia, Iran and North Korea, and estimates that 12 million TPP is roughly the maximum computing power of 750 Nvidia H100 or H200 chips.[^3]

The control also reaches indirect arrangements: the rule describes a customer outside the restricted group training or supplying models for a company in that group.[^2]
Foreign data centers are expected to check customers for direct or indirect ownership or control by people in the affected jurisdictions, and applications under the end-use control are reviewed case by case.[^2]
<!-- add role="reviser" model="OpenAI GPT-6 Astra" -->
The obligation can also arise after shipment: if a data center changes to a covered end-use, the operator making that transfer must obtain BIS authorization.[^2]
<!-- /add -->
The new remote-access control exempts computing power used exclusively for commercial recommender systems, such as those used by online retailers or social media companies, subject to reporting requirements.[^2]
<!-- add role="reviser" model="OpenAI GPT-6 Astra" -->
The definition excludes systems that generate text, images, audio, video, software or other content in response to inputs, including systems that also make recommendations.[^2]
<!-- /add -->

## Five destinations and an earlier framework

The rule also adds a license requirement for certain advanced semiconductor shipments to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil, which it describes as presenting a "higher-than-normal risk of diversion".[^2]
An exception applies to qualifying recipients headquartered in the United States or with a U.S.-headquartered ultimate parent, subject to reporting requirements.[^2]
The Associated Press reports that overseas data centers serving companies in countries including China, Russia, Iran and Cuba must identify their end users.[^4]

The rule formally rescinds the regulations implementing the Biden-era AI Diffusion Rule, which BIS says was never enforced and whose rescission it announced in May 2025.[^2]
The on-display rule says it takes effect on publication in the Federal Register but leaves that date as a placeholder, while AP reports that the rule took effect immediately.[^2][^4]
<!-- add role="reviser" model="OpenAI GPT-6 Astra" -->
Separately, the rule gives data centers already providing covered computing services before publication 90 days from its effective date to obtain a license if they want to continue, or face future export restrictions.[^2]

## The decisions the on-display rule leaves open

- The calendar deadline for existing operators remains unresolved in this document: its effective-date field is unfilled, so the transition period cannot yet be converted into a date from this text alone.[^2]
- Which operators will receive licenses is not specified: the rule sets case-by-case review rather than listing approved data centers.[^2]
- Additional destination controls remain possible: the rule allows further country designations but names no next country beyond the five listed.[^2]
<!-- /add -->

<!-- keypoints model="OpenAI GPT-6.1 Sol" -->
- Covered chip transfers require licenses above 12 million aggregate TPP supplied to firms headquartered in Macau or D:5 destinations, or whose ultimate parents are headquartered there.[^2]
- Certain chip shipments to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil need licenses; qualifying U.S.-headquartered recipients have a reporting-based exception.[^2]
- Existing covered data centers have a 90-day licensing transition; the displayed rule leaves its effective date unfilled despite AP reporting immediate effect.[^2][^4]
