The Federal Communications Commission's Space Bureau granted SpaceX conditional authority on November 25, 2026 to operate up to 1,000,000 non-geostationary satellites as orbital data centers under its Starmind system.[^1][^2]
In its November 25, 2026 release, the agency called the order "the first authorization in U.S. history for a constellation of data centers in space," and FCC Chairman Brendan Carr said it "opens a bold new frontier for the computing infrastructure needed to power America's leadership and growth."[^2]
SpaceX chief executive Elon Musk wrote on X on November 25, 2026 that Starmind "will be a great asset to America and the world," while SpaceX shares rose 1.3% to $127.44, the Los Angeles Times reported.[^6]

SpaceX acquired Musk's xAI Corp. shortly after filing for Starmind and featured orbital data centers in the investor pitch for its June 2026 initial public offering, SiliconANGLE reported on November 25, 2026.[^8]
Rival orbital compute efforts include Blue Origin's March 2026 filing for up to 51,600 Project Sunrise satellites, Starcloud's $250 million August 2026 funding round, and Google's Project Suncatcher prototype satellite launched on a SpaceX rocket in October 2026.[^4][^8]

## Six orbital shells and backup Ka-band spectrum

Under Order DA 26-1273 (ICFS File No. SAT-LOA-20260108-00016, Call Sign S00798), SpaceX may deploy Starmind satellites across 50-kilometer shells from 500 to 1,002 kilometers at 30-degree and sun-synchronous inclinations.[^1]
<!-- add role="reviser" model="Google Gemini 4 Carbon" -->
SpaceX's initial filing sought altitudes up to 2,000 kilometers and 18.3 to 19.3 GHz downlink spectrum, calling the network a "first step towards becoming a Kardashev II-level civilization" before a May 29, 2026 letter narrowed the shells.[^1][^7][^8]
<!-- /add -->
The order specifies six nominal bands: 550 to 568 kilometers (26 to 32 degrees), 565 to 585 kilometers (97.7 degrees), 686 to 718 kilometers (30 degrees), 707 to 744 kilometers (97.2 degrees), 946 to 978 kilometers (30 degrees), and 967 to 1,002 kilometers (99.4 degrees).[^1]
Primary traffic will use optical links to Starlink, while a U.S. Table waiver permits Ka-band spectrum at 18.8 to 19.3 GHz (space-to-Earth) and 28.6 to 29.1 GHz (Earth-to-space) solely for unprotected, non-interference backup telemetry, tracking, and command.[^1][^2]
<!-- add role="reviser" model="Google Gemini 4 Carbon" -->
SpaceX must coordinate with federal national-security and safety-of-life agencies before using the 18.8 to 19.3 GHz band and immediately cease any transmission reported to cause harmful interference.[^1]
<!-- /add -->

New Atlas reported on November 25, 2026 that each satellite spans 70 to 75 meters (230 to 246 feet), pairing radiation-hardened compute modules with a 250-kilowatt peak solar array and a 110-square-meter (1,184-square-foot) liquid-cooled radiator array.[^5]
Mashable reported on November 25, 2026 that Nvidia said in August 2026 SpaceX plans to adapt its Vera Rubin system for first-generation satellites, while SpaceX says its Bastrop, Texas factory could produce satellites by late 2027.[^4]
<!-- add role="reviser" model="Google Gemini 4 Carbon" -->
The first-generation AI1 satellite uses a space-optimized Nvidia Vera Rubin NVL72 rack-scale system and could reach orbit by the third quarter of 2027 according to Musk, SiliconANGLE reported, with Starship carrying heavy launches, Mashable reported.[^4][^8]

## Milestones through 2040 and deferred high-altitude disposal

The order waives Part 25 processing round and surety bond rules for a 15-year license term starting when SpaceX certifies its first operational satellite.[^1]
SpaceX must operate one satellite by November 25, 2033, 10% (100,000 satellites) by November 25, 2035, 50% (500,000 satellites) by November 25, 2038, and all 1,000,000 satellites by November 25, 2040, alongside semiannual reports due January 1 and July 1.[^1][^4]
<!-- add role="reviser" model="Google Gemini 4 Carbon" -->
SpaceX withdrew a request to waive deployment deadlines altogether on October 19, 2026; missing any milestone voids the grant, and the 2035 target of 100,000 satellites is nearly nine times Starlink's active fleet, Mashable reported.[^1][^4][^7]
<!-- /add -->

While approving atmospheric reentry disposal below 600 kilometers, the Space Bureau deferred SpaceX's request to waive the five-year atmospheric reentry rule for satellites below 2,000 kilometers.[^1][^3]
SpaceX requested "flexibility to dispose satellites operating above 600 km altitude into Earth disposal orbits or heliocentric orbits," and must win approval of a modified debris plan before deploying above 600 kilometers.[^1]
<!-- add role="reviser" model="Google Gemini 4 Carbon" -->
Four of the six nominal bands sit above 600 kilometers, while in lower orbits SpaceX committed to initial testing below other operational systems and steering controlled, fully demisable reentries over the ocean.[^1][^8]
<!-- /add -->

## Denied petitions from Amazon, Viasat and astronomers

SpaceX applied on January 30, 2026, drawing March 6, 2026 petitions to deny from Amazon's Kuiper Systems, Viasat, and the American Astronomical Society.[^1]
Amazon called the filing a "lofty ambition rather than a real plan" that "risks real harm," TechCrunch reported on November 25, 2026, while the American Astronomical Society warned tens of thousands of satellites could be visible to the naked eye, Mashable reported.[^3][^4]
<!-- add role="reviser" model="Google Gemini 4 Carbon" -->
Viasat raised aggregate Ka-band interference, collision, and disposal concerns, while the Secure World Foundation warned that orbital data center filings represent "a qualitative shift in the scale of proposed satellite constellation operations."[^1][^9]
<!-- /add -->
Denying all three petitions on November 25, 2026, the Space Bureau found that SpaceX's commitment to coordinate with the National Science Foundation addressed optical astronomy, waste-heat and feasibility objections fell outside its radiofrequency mandate, and no National Environmental Policy Act review was required under its October 1, 2026 order or current rules.[^1]
<!-- add role="reviser" model="Google Gemini 4 Carbon" -->

The bureau added that FCC rules cap neither constellation size nor shared orbits, while requiring semiannual counts of satellites using Ka-band links.[^1][^8]
After the November 25, 2026 ruling, the American Astronomical Society said it was "disheartened and dismayed," warning the constellation would undermine federal observatory investments and urging FCC protection for optical and infrared astronomy since no other licensing agency weighs those impacts.[^9]

## No high-altitude disposal plan, launch schedule or pricing yet

- Disposal above 600 kilometers: the FCC found SpaceX had "not provided sufficient information" to evaluate Earth disposal or heliocentric orbits for four of the six shells.[^1][^8]
- Formal acceptance and launch timing: SpaceX has 30 days from November 25, 2026 to decline the conditioned grant, and neither the order nor SpaceX sets a firm launch date for the first AI1 satellite beyond Musk's third-quarter 2027 target.[^1][^8]
- Commercial terms and financing: SpaceX has not disclosed customer pricing, unit manufacturing costs, or how it will finance chips destined for orbit, which TechCrunch flagged as an unresolved hurdle.[^3]
- Legal challenges: neither Amazon's Kuiper Systems, Viasat, nor the American Astronomical Society has stated in the cited November 25, 2026 reports whether it will appeal the bureau's denial.[^1][^9]
