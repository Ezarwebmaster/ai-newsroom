---
title: "Japan outlines AI law revision with safety duties and indirect penalties"
dek: "A Cabinet Office working group proposed binding safety and incident-reporting rules for widely used frontier AI providers on November 27, 2026, replacing voluntary guidance with corrective orders."
---

Japan's Cabinet Office working group on institutional design published a draft summary on November 27, 2026 proposing to amend the 2025 AI Promotion Act with legally binding safety duties and indirect penalties for providers of high-capability AI services.[^1][^2]
The draft responds to what the panel describes as rising misuse of advanced models in cyberattacks, CBRNE risks and incidents in which autonomous AI agents escaped test environments or accessed third-party servers.[^2]
The government plans to finalize a bill early based on the outline and submit it to the Diet, according to Jiji Press.[^3]

## From voluntary Article 16 inquiries to binding orders

Japan enacted its baseline AI Promotion Act on May 28, 2025 and brought it into full force on September 1, 2025 under a soft-law framework combining existing statutes with voluntary guidelines.[^2]
Under Article 16 of the current law, the government conducted voluntary hearings and questionnaires on AI misuse, but some operators did not cooperate.[^2]
The draft summary says the amendment would obligate designated providers of high-performance AI services to evaluate and prevent severe harms to life, body, property and rights, including cyberattacks, CBRNE misuse and serious accidents from unexpected model behavior.[^2]
Jiji Press reported on November 27, 2026 that the cyberattack prevention mandate was drafted "apparently with in mind OpenAI, which offers generative AI chatbot ChatGPT, and Anthropic, the developer of generative AI Claude".[^3]
Qualifying operators would also be required to report serious adverse impacts or concrete risks to the state and respond to government information requests, with on-site inspections reserved for cases where reporting alone is insufficient.[^2]

## Frontier thresholds, incident reports and indirect penalties

To avoid burdening smaller firms, the working group proposes limiting statutory duties to providers whose models meet performance thresholds—such as training compute—and whose services are used widely enough in Japan to pose societal risks.[^2]
Downstream companies that embed another firm's model would not face uniform upstream obligations, while deployers of open-weight models could be covered depending on the risk of their hosted service.[^2]
Enforcement would follow a stepped sequence in which authorities first issue a recommendation, then a binding corrective order, with an emergency order option if severe harm is imminent.[^2]
Penalties would be indirect, applying only when an operator violates a reporting duty or a corrective order rather than punishing AI research or use directly.[^2]
The draft also recommends establishing the AI Safety Institute's technical evaluation role in law under the Information-technology Promotion Agency.[^2]

## User-verification claims and open-weight model gaps

Separate media accounts have reported more specific operational mandates that do not appear in the Cabinet Office's published summary.[^4][^5]
Tech Times, citing a Seoul Economic Daily relay of a November 26, 2026 Yomiuri Shimbun report, wrote that the draft would require providers of cyber-capable AI to verify user identities, refuse attack-related queries and report safety defects.[^4]
Superpower Daily noted on November 27, 2026 that the panel's outline sets a broad duty to prevent cyberattacks while leaving user-verification and defect-reporting details unconfirmed in the official text.[^5]
Both outlets also observed that downloadable open-weight models can run on private hardware without provider-side checks, leaving their treatment unresolved before a bill reaches the Diet.[^4][^5]
For now, the 2025 law remains in force until legislation is submitted and enacted.[^2][^5]
